Teaching an AI a psychologist's framework, one honest number at a time

The expert, a psychologist, spent years mapping 12 coping styles in a 107,000-word manuscript. I'm building the system that reads what a person wrote and says which styles are at work, with her own labels as the yardstick. I designed the tool that produced those labels, then the scoring rules that decide what ships. The score went from 0.34 to 0.85 along the way, and one of my favorite results got retracted.

Ground truth

There was no dataset, so I built the tool that makes one

When I started in January 2026 there were five diagnosed cases. Six local models, from 8 to 120 billion parameters, all scored within half a point of each other on them. Five examples cannot teach anyone a 12-way distinction, model or human.

So I built the expert an annotation tool. It's a single HTML file. She imports statements, sees key phrases highlighted from the framework's own diagrams, adds style nodes, drags connections between them (core, wrapped, defense, and so on), and writes out her reasoning in her own words. A progress counter and a "next incomplete" button carry her through the set. There is no model prefill anywhere; the labels had to be hers. Forty-two versions later, the export held 79 labeled examples with one to five styles each.

Measurement

Every change earns a score before anyone gets an opinion

With 79 examples I could finally measure. Each candidate, whether a new prompt, a new model, or a new retrieval setting, diagnoses every statement, and its ranked styles are scored against the expert's. The score is F1: precision and recall combined into one number, where 1.0 is perfect. Then a written protocol decides what happens next.

  • Paired bootstrap on the same statements. A candidate replaces the champion only when P(better) ≥ 0.95.
  • Parse-OK ≥ 0.9. A model that answers well in the wrong format doesn't get scored on its good days.
  • Winners get re-run. A lucky result loses its shine the second time.
  • A 94-record regression suite, and every run committed to the repo. A conclusion has to cite its runs.
Retraction

Fine-tuning won. Then the math said it didn't, so I retracted it

In late June I got LoRA fine-tuning running on Apple Silicon and trained a small model on the 79 examples. It scored 0.222. The same model, merely prompted, scored 0.202. I wrote it up as a win.

Then I ran the paired bootstrap. p = 0.75. The same audit turned up a parser bug that had been inflating early scores. I retracted the claim in the log and marked the run. What fine-tuning on 79 examples actually bought was reliability: nearly every answer parsed, and it ran about three times faster. Accuracy didn't move.

The protocol went on to kill other favorites. Retrieval over the whole manuscript lost to a short prompt, 0.37 to 0.42. Generating more synthetic examples didn't help either.

What worked

What actually moved the number: better examples, retrieved per case

Rebuilding the prompt around the expert's examples took the best local model from 0.34 to 0.51. The next jump came from retrieval. For each new statement, the system pulls the eight most similar expert-labeled examples, reasoning attached, and puts them in front of the model. With a frontier model doing the reading, that reached 0.71.

On August 11 the current champion cleared the gate at P = 0.96, scoring 0.78 against the gold set. Ten days later I mined 107 real statements from a public corpus of a million posts and ran them cold. The candidate scored 0.85 on text nobody had tuned for.

Feedback loop

The model started correcting its teacher

On July 29 the expert sat down with every statement where the champion disagreed with her. She revised 17 of her 79 labels. Some disagreements were the model's mistakes. Others were hers, on statements labeled months earlier. The gold set got better because the model argued with it.

One caveat stays on the page. The gold set has a single annotator, so nobody knows how often two experts would agree. The measurable ceiling isn't 1.0, and I don't report it as if it were.

Where it runs

Local when it can be, frontier when it must

The research stack runs on one machine. People describing how they cope is sensitive text, and I wanted the offline option to be real. It is, and I measured what it costs: the two best local models tie at 0.68 and 0.66, about a tenth of a point behind the frontier model on the same examples.

For the companion app, that tenth matters. Production calls Claude through a provider-agnostic API, and the local models stay as the offline path. Either can be swapped without touching the rest.

Once the frontier model is reliably accurate it becomes the teacher, producing labeled examples and reasoning traces at a scale one expert never could, to train a small local model to do the same work faster and cheaper. An early probe with 161 teacher-written examples came out a tie, so for now that's a plan, not a result.

What I learned

Honest numbers are a feature

This project's log has a run marked INVALID, DO NOT CITE, a retracted headline, and a candidate that failed its gate and got parked. Those entries are what make the 0.85 worth anything. A system that knows how good it is can get better on purpose. One that doesn't can only get lucky.

This is a research and self-reflection tool grounded in the expert's framework. It is not a diagnostic instrument, and it makes no clinical claims.
